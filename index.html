01 / The problem

A newborn cannot wait three days for an answer.

Neonatal sepsis is a catastrophic immune response to a bloodstream infection in an infant less than 28 days old. It is one of the largest causes of newborn death in the world, and it is treatable — if you know what you are treating, and you know it quickly.

The problem is that finding out takes far too long. Conventional workflow means drawing blood, waiting for a culture to grow, sub-culturing to isolate the organism, then running susceptibility panels to learn which antibiotics will actually work. Two to three days, start to finish. Meanwhile the clinician has to guess — and every guess is a bet placed against a clock.

That delay is not evenly distributed. The burden falls hardest on low- and middle-income countries, where the instruments that could shorten it are too expensive, too fragile, or too dependent on infrastructure that isn't there.

02 / What we're building

One instrument. Three answers. Straight from whole blood.

We are developing a single, robust, low-cost instrument that collapses the entire diagnostic chain into one run — no separate culture step, no sub-culture, no second panel.

It works by watching. Advanced digital imaging captures bacterial behaviour at a scale and cadence a human eye can't resolve, and signal processing pulls the growth signature out of that data long before a colony would ever become visible. Deep learning does the reading. The result is phenotypic — a direct measurement of how the organism actually responds to each drug, not an inference from its genome.

01 — Detection

Is there an infection?

Optical detection of bacterial presence in a whole blood sample, without waiting on a positive culture flag.

02 — Identification

What organism is it?

Pathogen identification from imaging signatures, narrowing the field from "some bloodstream infection" to a named species.

03 — Susceptibility

Which drug will work?

Phenotypic antimicrobial susceptibility testing — measured drug response, at FDA-level performance targets, across a wide pathogen range.

03 / Time to result

Days become hours.

Bars are drawn to scale. At a 7.6% mortality increase per hour of delay, the gap between these two lines is not a convenience — it is the entire clinical argument.

04 / The company

Small team, unreasonable problem.

AstraDx is a biomedical diagnostics company in Beverly, Massachusetts, building high-performance, low-cost diagnostic instruments for use all over the world.

"Low-cost" is the hard constraint, and it is deliberate. A diagnostic that only works in a well-funded tertiary hospital does not touch the population carrying most of the burden. Every design decision we make — optics, consumables, compute — is made against the question of whether the finished instrument can plausibly be deployed where it is needed most.
